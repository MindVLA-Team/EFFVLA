What Makes an Efficient VLA?

Navigating Action-Head Design,
Scaling, and Latency

Abstract

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models combine a pretrained vision encoder, a language backbone, and an action head, but which of these design choices actually drives performance has not been measured under controlled, latency-paired conditions. We fix the backbone (SigLIP2-So400m and Qwen2.5) and the pipeline (DROID, LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus), and sweep the action head along four axes — decoder, loss, initialization, inference passes — and the per-module scale, pairing every configuration with measured on-device latency.

Three findings come out of the experiment. First, what decides the action head is not its decoder, loss, or inference budget but how it is initialized. Copying the last transformer layers of the language backbone into the head is the single largest lever we measure, raises accuracy at no latency cost, and is the only axis that pays at every module scale we test. It also reorders the rest of the design space: flow matching, extra inference passes, and a heavier decoder pay off only while the head is randomly initialized and stop paying once it is not, as if the expressive machinery were compensating for something the initialized head already has. We read that something as representation transfer, and offer it as the account that best organizes the measurements rather than as a demonstrated cause. Second, capacity only pays after alignment: the action head is the highest-return module to scale, but only once it is aligned. Third, those returns saturate at a model size close to the one today's π-series VLAs already use, so growing the model further only adds latency.

The three together specify EffVLA, a compact model that matches the strongest open-source VLAs on standard LIBERO, leads on most LIBERO-Plus perturbation axes at lower latency, and transfers to a real SO-ARM101 arm with the recipe unchanged.

Three findings

What the measurement says

Finding 1 · what to build

Align the head, then keep it simple

What the head is short of is alignment with the VLM, not expressive capacity. Initialization supplies it: +7.1 points at no latency cost. Once aligned, flow matching loses 4.4; a fourth pass and a heavier decoder fall within noise.

Finding 2 · where to scale

Capacity only pays after alignment

Aligned, the action head returns ~4 success points per added millisecond, against ~1 for the vision encoder and ~0.15 for the language backbone. Misaligned, the same scaling buys about a third as much (+3.1 vs +9.4).

Finding 3 · how big

Stop at a modest size

On LIBERO-Plus, scaling any module past the l configuration leaves accuracy flat while latency keeps climbing. The ~3.75B budget — close to the π-series scale — is the efficiency/accuracy knee.

The design space

Eight substrates, one backbone

A substrate is a fixed choice of the four action-head axes, independent of module size. All eight share the same SigLIP2 + Qwen2.5 backbone and read it through the same KV-share interface — they differ only in the action head.

Everything that usually moves between VLA papers is held still: same backbone, same DROID → LIBERO pipeline, same LIBERO-Plus protocol (four task suites × seven zero-shot perturbation axes, ~1,000 rollouts per cell), same hardware for latency (RTX 5090, bf16, batch 1, chunk size 8, torch.compile). Any difference below has to come from the head.

The result that reorganized the paper

Every expressive trick works — until you align the head

Take the same four axes and read each one twice: once on a head initialized at random, once on a head initialized from the backbone. The decoder and loss axes reverse sign. Alignment never does.

Every axis that reverses is a way of adding expressive capacity to the head — a generative loss, a second pass, more decoder machinery — and each earns its keep only while the head is initialized at random. Once initialization supplies what a random head lacks, none of that machinery has anything left to contribute, and it becomes pure latency. What the head is short of is not expressiveness. It is alignment.

The mechanism

Alignment is not a metaphor — it is measurable

If alignment is really the variable, it should be visible in the weights and in the behaviour, not just in the success rates. It is, in both.

In weight space. Measuring linear CKA between the action head and the corresponding backbone layers, the aligned head holds 0.76 with the backbone throughout fine-tuning while a random head never rises above 0.24. The separation holds at every layer of the head, and the success-rate advantage holds across all six vision- and language-backbone sizes. Read per module, the K+V and output projections are preserved most (CKA 0.79 and 0.81) while the query path adapts most (0.54): the head keeps what the backbone reads and re-purposes how it attends.

In behaviour. Told to “put the black bowl in the bottom drawer of the cabinet,” the aligned head concentrates on the instruction and on the bowl it names, while the random head spreads attention diffusely across the scene and leaves the instruction almost unattended.

And it is not one lucky rollout. Across all 128 rollouts the aligned head places roughly four times as much attention on the instruction (0.233 vs 0.055), and the two distributions do not overlap on a single rollout. Ranked by attention, the words it looks at are the object and location nouns that name the task.

An honest caveat. We manipulate alignment only through initialization, so this evidence explains the measurements rather than proving causation. The decisive control — initializing from the same backbone weights with the alignment destroyed (permuted layers, or a random orthogonal rotation) — would collapse both the CKA and the gain if alignment is what matters. We flag it as future work in the paper rather than claiming the causal link.

Then capacity

Capacity only pays after alignment — and then it stops

Once the head is aligned it becomes the single highest-return place to spend compute. Misaligned, the same parameters buy about a third as much.

Added capacity on an aligned head extends a computation the backbone already shares; on a misaligned head it must first spend itself relearning the interface. And it stops early: a 1B vision encoder scores 78.9, a 7B language backbone 77.8, a 650M head 78.3 — all at or below the 79.8 anchor, while latency only climbs.

The recipe

EffVLA — what the three rules select

The analysis specifies the model, not the other way round: a 4-layer transformer-block head copied from the last 4 layers of Qwen2.5-3B, trained with single-pass L1 regression, reading the backbone through a KV-share interface on a SigLIP2-So400m encoder.

Of ~3.75B parameters the head adds only ~0.35B, and it runs in 39.2 ms per action chunk. Nothing in the architecture is tuned beyond what the three rules prescribe.

It transfers to a real arm

The recipe transfers unchanged to a low-cost SO-ARM101 open-source 6-DOF arm — same head, same loss, no axis of the study re-tuned for the new embodiment. Only the data pipeline changes: Stage A uses the SO-ARM101 community corpus in place of DROID, and Stage B fine-tunes on a small set of teleoperated demonstrations. Across five language-specified sorting tasks it succeeds on 40 of 50 trials.

We report this as a feasibility and transfer result — we did not run the alternative action heads on hardware, so it does not show that the LIBERO-selected recipe stays preferable in the physical setting.

Does the effect hold outside this grid?

Two checks, neither of which extends the claim: they test whether the one result the paper rests on is specific to the configuration we happened to sweep.

It holds in every matched pair. Ten pairs of cells in the study differ only in how the action head was initialized, holding decoder, parameter count, latency, module scales and inference budget fixed. All ten favour VLM-init, by +5.8 points on average, across eight module-scale configurations, both inference budgets, and two benchmarks.

It reproduces on another embodiment. On SimplerEnv (WidowX), the same contrast is +7.3 points against +7.1 on LIBERO-Plus — and there the random-init head is architecture-matched: the same Qwen decoder layers are deep-copied and then re-drawn, so topology, parameter count and latency are identical and only the weight values differ. That places the gain in the weights, not in the head's architecture or capacity.
